MS Thesis in Computer Science, University of Minnesota

Does a more realistic virtual evaluator cause more stress in VR?

System built, waiting for IRB approval. Data collection planned for Spring 2027.

Overview

You are doing mental math against the clock while someone across the table watches and takes notes. Does it matter how real that person looks? This thesis tests that in virtual reality.

Participants sit at a desk in a virtual meeting room and answer on a tablet. After a quiet untimed block, an evaluator walks in, sits down and stays for the timed block. One group gets a highly realistic MetaHuman, one gets a simpler Rocketbox avatar, and one gets nobody.

The two evaluators share the same movements, voice, room and task, so the comparison is about appearance. Stress is measured from the body and from what participants report.

What stays the same

The comparison only means something if the two evaluators differ in appearance and nothing else. This is what the design holds fixed.

Identical in both evaluator groups

  • Recorded body motion and timing
  • Voice and the two spoken lines
  • Where the evaluator looks, and when
  • Room, desk, lighting and sounds
  • Task, questions and feedback
  • Clothing style
  • Apparent age, gender and ethnicity
  • No conversation with the evaluator

The task

Mental arithmetic in the style of the Montreal Imaging Stress Task, with addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. Difficulty follows the person across eight levels. In the timed block each question has a limit between 3 and 12 seconds that tightens after a correct answer and relaxes after a miss. The screen shows feedback and running accuracy.

Checking the manipulation

Participants rate how real the evaluator looked and behaved and how aware they were of it. Timed and untimed blocks are compared within each person to confirm the task itself raises stress.

Running it in the headset

  • PC VR. Unreal Engine runs on the PC and streams to the Quest 3 over Quest Link, using OpenXR, DirectX 12 and DLSS upscaling.
  • Hand tracking. Participants answer by touching the virtual keypad with a fingertip, with no controllers.
  • Cheaper lighting. Profiling showed shadows taking most of each frame, so the room lights were made static and the face lights cast no shadows.
  • One avatar at a time. Only the evaluator for the participant's group is kept in the scene during a session.
  • A checked task engine. The difficulty and time limit rules are tested against a separate Python version, with automated tests.

What was difficult

Two different bodies

The avatars have different skeletons and proportions. After retargeting, hands, pen and head had to be fitted again so both do the same thing in the same place.

Contact with the desk

Recorded motion does not know where the table is. Hands, forearms and the pen had to stay on the clipboard and desk, not pass through them.

Looking at the right thing

Recorded head and eye movements often missed their targets in the virtual room, so gaze was aimed again at the participant, the screen and the paper.

Eight minutes from short takes

The seated performance is assembled from separate recordings, which had to join without jumps or obvious repetition.

Matching the look

The Rocketbox avatar had no beard and different clothes, so its textures were repainted and revised several times.

Failures that only show in a session

Test runs found cases that would have stalled a participant, such as the questions running out, and these were fixed.

Analysis plan

Planned before any data is collected, so the results cannot shape the analysis.

  • Baseline correction. Physiological measures are compared as change from each person's own 3 minute baseline.
  • Group comparisons. t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests depending on the data, and regression with the exit survey measures as covariates.
  • Skin conductance responses. Response counts are modeled with a negative binomial model.
  • Manipulation check. Timed versus untimed blocks within each person, to confirm the task raises stress at all.
  • Reading a null result. A non-significant result is reported as no difference detected, not as no difference. A Bayesian equivalence analysis is possible.
